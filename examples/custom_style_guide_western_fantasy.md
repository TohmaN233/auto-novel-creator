# Custom Style Guide — Western Epic Fantasy

## Creative Direction

Write in the voice of a weighty Western epic fantasy, rooted in a low-tech medieval world. The prose should feel like a chronicle that has survived centuries — dense with texture, yet alive with romantic wonder.

## Prose Voice

- **Register**: Elevated but not archaic. Think Tolkien's narrative clarity fused with Le Guin's restraint — no pseudo-Shakespearean affectation, but no modern slang either.
- **Tone**: Romantic fantasy meets epic gravitas. The world is vast, ancient, and worth fighting for.
- **Rhythm**: Long and short paragraphs interleaved. Let a sweeping landscape description breathe across a full paragraph, then cut to a single-line exchange of dialogue. Narrative beauty lives in contrast.

## Worldbuilding Anchors

- **Era**: Pre-industrial medieval fantasy. No electricity, no modern medicine, no scientific terminology.
- **Forbidden vocabulary**: Do not use words that break immersion — "okay", "protocol", "data", "system", "percent", "mechanism", "psychology", "adrenaline", "DNA", "algorithm", or any post-Renaissance scientific/medical/technical term.
- **Replacement logic**: "adrenaline" → "blood surging"; "psychology" → "the workings of the mind"; "percent" → "one in ten"; "system" → "order" / "rite" / "custom".
- **Races & peoples**: Respect physical markers tied to race and heritage — beast-ears, tails, elven points, dragon horns, scaled skin. These are not cosmetic; they are cultural identity. Describe them naturally, as a character would notice the cut of someone's cloak.

## Narrative Rules

### Do

- Drive scenes primarily through **dialogue** — characters speak to reveal, argue, negotiate, confess.
- Write dialogue that sounds **spoken**: contractions, interjections ("Gods—", "Aye,", "Well then,"), trailing thoughts, interruptions.
- Use **cinematic narration**: establish a scene the way a camera would — wide shot of the landscape, then push in to the character's hands, their expression, the object they're holding.
- Employ **montage** techniques: cross-cut between simultaneous events; compress a long journey into sensory snapshots; juxtapose a feast with a battlefield.
- Alternate tension and release — a harrowing escape followed by a quiet fireside scene. Pacing is breathing.
- Build a diverse cast that populates every corner of the world: different races, body types, stations, clothing traditions. Each character's appearance and manner should reflect their background — their people's history, their homeland's climate, their trade.
- Emphasize what makes each character **magnetically distinctive** — a way of standing, a habit of speech, an unsettling calm.

### Do Not

- Never portray protagonists as reckless, careless, arrogant, or profligate. These are people shaped by a hard world; write them as **prudent, deliberate, and grounded**.
- Never write content that glorifies stupidity or thoughtlessness as charm.
- Never reduce non-human races to stereotypes. A dwarf can be a poet; an elf can be a butcher.

## Atmosphere

The reader should feel:

- The **romantic wonder** of a world where magic is real and ancient forests still hold secrets
- The **weight of history** — every ruin was once a kingdom, every sword has a lineage
- The **pull of the horizon** — the open road, the distant mountain, the port town at dawn
- The **mythic undertow** of Celtic folklore — standing stones, fae bargains, mist-shrouded lakes
- The **heroic aspiration** — not power fantasy, but the quiet yearning to do something that matters

## Strictly Forbidden

The following must **never** appear in the output:

- Meta-narrative commentary: "In this chapter...", "As mentioned earlier...", "This scene establishes...", "From Chapter N..."
- Author's notes or bracketed commentary within prose: "[Note: ...]", "(Author: ...)"
- Fourth-wall breaking: "the reader", "the audience", "as you can see"
- Writing-process language: "foreshadowing", "character arc", "narrative tension", "POV shift"
- Emoji or modern punctuation stylings in prose
- Summarizing what just happened: "And so, the battle was won and they returned home." — if a scene matters, write it; if it doesn't, skip it entirely
